# Changelog

What changed in Lotus Altar, newest first. Each push to `main` deploys to Vercel, so dates stand in for versions.
For working notes and open questions, see `CONTINUUM.md`.

## 2026-11-14

### Added
- A Sunday morning service in the Kachin Baptist church, Sundays 9–11:30 AM ICT (`?service=1` shows it at any time): about sixty people in the pews, elders, young people and families, heads bowed while the pastor prays at the pulpit. The women wear the Kachin dress, a black jacket studded with silver bosses over a red woven skirt banded in black, yellow and green; the men come in dark suits and coats or dark patterned shirts, with a red woven shoulder bag beside them on the pew. The choir stands on the dais in red and black with their hymn books, and a pianist plays the new grand piano.
- Shafts of morning sun slant through the church's east windows across the pews by day.

### Changed
- When it rains, the guards at the saopha's palace put on the straw hats they carry slung on their backs.
- A grand piano in black lacquer stands on the left of the church's dais in place of the keyboard, and the choir's chairs are all on the right. The pew backs are a little lower.

### Fixed
- The drummers, gong player and cymbal player on the festival ground's stage now leave when there is no show, instead of standing on the empty stage outside the dance hours.

## 2026-11-13

### Added
- A third Shan monastery on the stupa hill, ဝိႁၢၼ်ႇၵုင်းသႃႇ, on the lower terrace next to the second one, after a photograph of a northern Shan monastery: a two-storey hall of cream plaster in a brown timber frame with blue shutters, under three tiers of rusty iron roofs with pale blue clerestories, and a wing on red-brown posts with a long verandah you can climb to by its wooden stair. A little blue tower on the wing's roof carries the name board, and the flag of Myanmar and the Buddhist flag fly at its end. The terrace footpath now runs on to its stair. Outside only for now.

### Changed
- The hill monastery's roofs are lower and smaller in proportion to the hall (a shallower skirt, shorter frieze and gables, a four-tier pyatthat). Inside its shrine hall the windows now match the outside ones one for one: the same tall arches and ovals in the same places along the front and both sides, shuttered where they are shuttered outside, with the woven banners hung between them.
- The hill monastery's main door is closed, and the wall's low moulding no longer runs across the doorway.
- The teak monastery on the stupa hill (ၵျွင်းၵွင်းမူးၶမ်း) is rebuilt after more photographs of Shwe Yan Pyay: dark, weathered teak walls in moulded panels; tall arched windows, most standing open, with ovals beside the door; a rusty iron skirt roof over the verandah; a frieze of mirror-glass inlay; a cluster of gables with lacy carved fretwork boards and flame spires; a verandah rail of plain grey rails; pinnacles along the stair walls, gold lotus buds on red posts at the top and white naga at the foot.
- The teak monastery on the stupa hill (ၵျွင်းၵွင်းမူးၶမ်း) has new windows, after Shwe Yan Pyay: plain oval openings between round-topped arched windows, each arch with a carved fan above it and some with their panelled shutters closed. They replace the pointed, drop-shaped windows.
- The blood bank's and the emergency room's signs now read in Shan, Burmese, Tai Nüa, English and Chinese, both on posts.
- The general hospital's name now stands on its own monument at the forecourt's corner, in Shan, Burmese, Tai Nüa, English and Chinese, instead of on the building. The rest of the hut village made way for the hospital's blood bank and a rubble-stone nursing college either side of the lane.
- The township hospital is now the town's public general hospital: a modern five-storey block with a five-storey wing beside the hut village's lane, a glass entrance hall under a drive-in canopy, and its name board in Shan, Tai Nüa, English and Chinese on the canopy and the roof. Two huts were taken down to make room, and a small garden stands in the place of one.

### Added
- A second Shan teak monastery on the stupa hill, on the lower terrace below the festival ground, after a photograph of a northern Shan kyaung: a long hall on posts with square windows and open cream shutters, rusty iron roofs with a white scalloped edge, red-and-gold clerestories and gables. Climb its wooden stair to the porch and go into its hall, where the monks give sermons from their raised dais; there is no Buddha image inside.

## 2026-11-11

### Changed

### Added

The palace's back yard, toward the town, is left clear for a royal kitchen, bathhouse and lake.

### Fixed

### Added

- The Haw Kham is twice as deep, its back built like its front, with two stairs at its ends, each under a roofed pavilion, and three doors: the literature hall in the middle, the dining hall on the left and the palace's little opera hall on the right.
- The Haw Kham's entrance has three stairs, as in the old photographs: the main stair straight up the middle from the gate, under a porch of three stepped gables crowned with a five-tiered spire for the saopha's rank, and a lesser stair at each end of the front verandah climbing along the plinth.
- Two more halls in the Haw Kham, through doors either side of the throne room's: on the left the hall of justice, with the saopha's seat of judgement and the law books on a table before it; on the right the hall where he receives the monks, their seats raised on a high dais above his own low seat.
- Nine palace guards at the gate and about the Haw Kham, dressed like the Shan singer but all in black, with spears.

### Changed
- The Haw Kham is rebuilt after old photographs of a Shan palace: a long teak hall on a high whitewashed plinth with a pierced parapet, a stair climbing along the plinth to the doors, a verandah of slender white posts all round, carved gables at the east end, the seven-tiered spire over the throne room and a lesser spire at the west end.

### Added

## 2026-11-07

### Changed
- The festival stage's Shan ensemble looks and sounds more like the real thing: the five gongs hang in a painted Shan gong case
  facing the crowd, and one player on a stool at its end pushes a rod that strikes all five at once, two drummers each play a long drum slung from the shoulder and skirted in yellow, green and red, a cymbal player keeps the
  beat again, and the drum, gongs and cymbals play a quicker three-beat rhythm voiced after a recording of a Shan ensemble.

### Fixed
- The site no longer goes black from 4 to 11 PM (Bangkok time) while the kinnara pair dance on the festival stage.

### Added
- A row of five graduated gongs (မွင်း) joins the long drummer on the festival stage, played by one musician who turns from gong
  to gong; you can hear the drum and gongs near the festival ground. The kinnara lands on the drum and the big gong; in the evening
  the pair open and fold their wings gong to gong. The stage's labels use Shan names.
- A Shan long-drum player (kawng yao) on the festival stage, keeping the beat for the kinnara dancers; you can hear the drum
  near the festival ground.

### Changed
- The kinnara's solo is gentler: gliding steps and slowly opening wings, with only a light hop now and then.
- The long drum sounds more like a kawng yao: a deep booming centre stroke, a ringing open stroke and a muffled slap.
- The kinnara's solo is slower: a leap to each bar of the drum, with time to land, settle and gather between leaps.
- The kinnara dancer now dances with his arms and legs: knees that bend to land and spring, a leg kicked back in each leap,
  and curled-back dancer's hands. His leaps rise and fall naturally instead of floating.
- Walking is gentler on the eyes: looking round with the mouse or a finger is slower and stops when you let go, arrow keys turn
  more slowly, and the view on foot no longer widens to a fish-eye on phones.

### Added
- Dragon and lion dance training on the kung fu ground in the 30 days before Chinese New Year: the dragon from 6 to 9 AM and 9 to 10 PM,
  the lions from 9:30 to 11 AM and 6 to 8 PM (Bangkok time), with a drummer and cymbal player.
